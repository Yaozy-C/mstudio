# Craft and revision

Optional tools for the direction at hand. Not every piece needs a surprise ending.

## Write for viewing pleasure

| Direction | Writing focus | Common mistakes |
|---|---|---|
| Satisfaction, process, ASMR | Initial state, action rhythm and change, tactile sound, completed state; the payoff can keep arriving | Listing objects; a gentle music bed instead of sound design |
| Demonstration, test, tutorial | A question worth answering, a clear operation, a readable result; the conclusion matches the scale of what was shown | Slogans instead of proof; capabilities the material does not support |
| Life record, UGC | How a specific person chooses and uses the product; real details, natural speech, a personal view | A generic actor labelled with a profession; selling points read aloud |
| Comedy, character story | What the character wants, the situation, reactions, relationships, development | Manufactured trouble; delaying an answer the audience already guessed |
| Emotional piece | Relationships revealed through behaviour, the cost of a choice, the weight of a small action | Declaring the emotion in narration, then matching it with staged shots |
| Visual discovery, stylised ad | Perceptible rules, anomalies, change and revelation; image and sound carry the meaning | Only camera moves and transitions; the product pasted on at the end |

Choose details that carry meaning instead of piling up adjectives. One action can convey both character and product; a simple process gains style from composition, the choice of actions and sound. A metaphor may be unrealistic, but it must never pose as a demonstration of product performance.

## An idea is not a title

To pin a vague concept down, write it in a few sentences: "At the start we see or hear ___; then ___, so / but / while ___; finally ___. The audience gets its payoff at ___; the product is understood at ___."

This is a clarifying tool, not a story structure. A process piece can be organised by sensory change; an explanatory piece can give the answer first and then demonstrate; a story can skip the setup and start in action. Within one direction compare different expressions, and within one expression compare different claims; one version of an opening is not a whole strategy.

## Three micro-editing exercises

Fictional exercises, not proven hits, and not applied to any client product by default.

**Process: tidying cables.** Weak draft: "Show the desk, put down the charger, put down the cable, pull the zip, show the organiser pouch." Specific choices: "Three cables lie tangled on the desk; a hand lifts one and the other two come up with it. After they are separated, each is looped once and placed in its own slot, whose existence was established earlier; the three plugs face the same way and drop in one after another. The pouch closes; only the one cable that is charging is left on the desk." The change lies in the relationship from chaos to order and in the payoff of the action; no late-arriving character is needed. If the individual slots are not evidenced, do not write them.

**Emotion: leaving dinner for a roommate.** Weak draft: "Prepare dinner, write a caring note, warm ending." Specific choices: "Serve two portions of noodles; as the coriander is about to be sprinkled, the spoon stops over the second portion, then the coriander goes into a small box placed beside it." The action lets the audience infer a memory of another person's taste. If the product is only background, this is an emotional fragment, not yet an ad: find an evidenced product relationship or drop the direction.

**Demonstration: drawer storage.** Weak draft: "Can never find anything? This storage box solves it in one move!" Specific choices: "First show the small accessory to be found, then the drawer holding many accessories, so the search and the moment of taking it out are visible." The question becomes testable. Whether a comparison, a timer or an explanation is needed depends on the brief and a real demonstration; never fabricate a speed difference or promise that the product improves memory.

## Fix a flat piece by locating the problem

Quote one symptom from the script, then make the smallest effective fix:

- No focus: several values promised at once. Choose the main expression; reorganise or cut the side lines.
- No material: everything is convenient, refined, healing. Add real behaviour, object choices, actual words or sensory detail.
- No payoff: only a completed operation. Make clear which moment lets the audience discover, learn, feel or enjoy something.
- Stagnation: after the second segment only the known repeats. Compress, change the order of revelation, or give the repetition a variation.
- Forced: characters act foolish for the ad. Switch to a credible situation or a direct demonstration.
- Disconnect: selling starts only after the interesting part ends. Reconnect experience and product; if they cannot connect, change direction.
- Dragging: the idea works but preparation and dialogue drown it. Keep the recognition time that is necessary; cut explanations and transitions.

Separate personal taste, a violated user constraint, a product-fact error, and an audio-visual that cannot express the intent. An aesthetic preference is not an objective defect.

## What a review can and cannot tell you

Reading your own draft tests expressibility, coherence, facts and whether assumptions are marked. It cannot predict views or conversions, and a high self-assessment proves nothing. Deliver once the justified revisions are made.
