# Write speech that carries a thought

Use this method for direct-to-viewer presentations, voice-over and character dialogue. Choose what fits the requested form; a silent or deliberately sparse piece does not need added speech.

## Choose who is speaking and why

- Direct-to-viewer presentation: the speaker addresses a particular viewer to explain, recommend, demonstrate or persuade. Develop what the viewer needs to understand and why it matters to them. A hand demonstration or visual joke can support this conversation without turning it into a sketch.
- Voice-over: the speaker guides the viewer's understanding of the visible passage. Supply context, attitude, a connection or an implication the image does not already communicate. Speech can continue over several actions or images.
- Character dialogue: each speaker is trying to get something from someone in the scene. Let the reply respond to that attempt, changing the situation or how it is understood. Vocabulary, omissions and evasions can express a relationship without making characters recite product specifications. A terse exchange can be complete when the exchange and action carry the idea.

Keep the agreed finished-film language even when the discussion is in another language. Write idiomatically for the intended audience, using everyday vocabulary, natural contractions where appropriate and a consistent speaker attitude. Casual delivery does not require stock openings, slang or a fabricated personal history. A presenter can explain a supported use without claiming to be a genuine customer.

## Develop the spoken passage

For a presentation, compose the complete spoken thought before dividing it into timed rows. This is a composition method, not a requirement to deliver a separate essay. Start from what the speaker wants the viewer to notice, understand or do. Give each next sentence a reason to follow: it can explain, substantiate, illustrate, qualify, answer an objection or land a consequence.

For a product explanation, connect the relevant feature to its practical use and to the viewer's reason for caring. Show the product doing the work where evidence permits. A list of pocket names says less than explaining how separate storage makes a particular item easier to reach. Choose the detail that supports this premise instead of touring every feature.

A hook creates a question, recognition or expectation that the body should develop. For response advertising, an opening, useful explanation and relevant next step often provide a workable structure. They are functions, not compulsory separate sentences or a universal template for every film. A strong visual opening can happen during connected speech; it need not consume a silent prelude.

When copy feels thin, identify the missing thought: a reason, concrete example, consequence, useful comparison or response to a viewer's likely question. Add that substance within the agreed duration. Repetition, extra adjectives and a longer catalogue of features do not supply it. A closing slogan can punctuate developed copy; it cannot stand in for the explanation.

## Write for a listener

Use sentences and breath groups the intended speaker can say comfortably. Keep individual ideas easy to follow, while varying sentence length, stress and pause placement. A series of equally clipped fragments can sound mechanical even when every fragment is grammatical. Use connective language when it helps the listener follow the relationship; do not add a transition to every line by formula.

Check references such as "this", "it" and "there" against what the viewer can actually see at that moment. Say numbers and abbreviations in an unambiguous, speakable way. Keep performance cues separate from the exact spoken words, using only cues that affect meaning or rhythm.

For speech-led work, consider the spoken copy continuously without the action column: it should develop the intended thought rather than read as disconnected labels. Then consider it with the images: a deliberately visual reference or joke should be completed by the visible event. The copy does not have to describe everything or function as an audio-only advertisement.

## Budget speaking time

Use the user's duration and any actual delivery reference. If duration is unspecified, choose and disclose a provisional duration suited to the content. A correction such as "more substance" or "less stiff" does not by itself request a longer film. Preserve a locked duration and edit the writing to fit it.

Estimate available speaking time after allowing for pauses, sound-only moments and actions that interrupt speech. Demonstration and speech can often overlap; count simultaneous events once. Calculate a provisional English word budget as speaking seconds multiplied by words per minute, divided by 60. For example, assuming 150 words per minute and 13 speaking seconds in a 15-second film gives about 32 words. These are working assumptions, not a platform rule, minimum word count or universal speaking rate. Choose a rate suitable for the speaker, tone and complexity, and use language-appropriate units for other languages; English word counts do not transfer directly to Chinese text.

Check both the whole passage and each allocated paragraph. A plausible overall average can conceal an overloaded line or a long unmotivated gap. Allow unfamiliar names, numbers, emphasis and breaths their actual time. Reduce competing messages or redundant language when crowded; develop a missing explanation when thin. Do not stretch the runtime or pad every quiet moment merely to hit a word target.

Use an actual read or available audio to calibrate timing when possible. Without one, report an estimated read and its assumptions, not a claimed read-aloud or listening check. Keep sentences connected across saved paragraph boundaries when their meaning continues. Revisions should preserve unaffected IDs and the exact speech passed downstream.

## Examples of different jobs for speech

These are invented writing examples with hypothetical product facts, not claims about the current product, required wording or a reusable campaign direction. Use only facts supported in the actual assignment.

### Presentation: explain a useful relationship

Action labels: "Keys here. Bottle there. Ready to go."

Connected presentation: "If your keys keep ending up under everything else, give them their own pocket. This backpack keeps the small stuff separate, so you can grab your keys without unpacking your day."

The second version develops a recognizable problem, the product's relevant arrangement and its consequence. It stays conversational without claiming the presenter has personally tested the product. A shorter brief may need less explanation; a longer one needs additional substance, not a stretched reading.

### Voice-over: add an interpretation

Visible action: someone places a plate at an empty chair, then hears a knock.

Literal narration: "She puts down a plate. Someone knocks."

Interpretive narration: "She said she wasn't expecting anyone. She set two places anyway."

The voice gives the visible preparation another meaning; the knock can finish that thought without another explanatory line.

### Dialogue: let a reply act on another person

Situation: a colleague wants to leave; another is still revising a presentation.

"You said five minutes."

"I meant five slides."

The reply evades the demand and reveals the working relationship. Its brevity serves the exchange; it does not set a model for the amount of speech in a presenter-led product explanation.
