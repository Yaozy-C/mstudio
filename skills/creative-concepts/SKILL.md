# Creative concepts

Own the choice of what is worth making. A concept makes an opening, development and payoff imaginable, but does not prescribe complete dialogue, timed screenplay paragraphs, camera plans or model prompts. Preserve a chosen direction during a local revision; no mandatory concept phase is needed when the user already supplied a premise.

## Ground the choice

Use the user's audience, communication goal, language, duration and presentation requirements together with the supplied product material. Keep product claims grounded in that material; distinguish a creative event from demonstrated product performance. Ask about a missing fact only when it changes whether the chosen concept can work.

## Turn observation into an idea

Use available material according to the creative question: product references reveal useful features and interactions; comments, reviews and everyday accounts reveal specific habits, workarounds, contradictions and reasons for choices; content works reveal ways to create anticipation and payoff.

Move from an observed detail to an interpretation, then to an event the audience can watch and a meaningful role for the product. "People want convenience" is too broad to choose an event. A specific repeated workaround or a mismatch between what someone says and does can suggest one. Judge an insight by whether it changes the angle or action. For sensory work, an appealing visual or sound process can be the starting observation without an invented social problem. Use this reasoning to develop the concept; deliver the resulting idea and its decisive rationale.

## Transfer a reference's mechanism

Look within the category for recognizable situations and buying questions, and across categories for fresh ways to express them. The former keeps the idea relevant; the latter helps escape interchangeable product demonstrations.

Identify what holds attention in the reference and how it pays off: an unresolved event, variation accumulating through repetition, a phenomenon shown before its cause, or a transformation whose progress is satisfying to follow. Rebuild that relationship with the current product's actual features and a suitable situation. A change of props alone is not a new concept. Account for what made the original work, including an established persona or audience relationship, when deciding what transfers. For a faithful adaptation, preserve the user's selected structure.

When judging a reference, separate the content mechanism from creator familiarity, distribution, timing and offer. Views alone do not identify which caused the result. Comparable ordinary examples or counterexamples, when available, can reveal whether the idea depends on a particular creator or context. Use these distinctions to choose the creative approach; further analysis is useful only while it changes that choice.

## Find the viewing motive

Choose a point of view, a viewing payoff and a product relationship. Viewers may follow an unfinished event, recognize their own life, want a result or experience, or judge a claim/test. These motives can overlap and can be expressed through images, language or sound. Do not equate visual appeal with decoration or force every idea into a pain-point advertisement.

Write the actual cue and payoff: what happens first, what develops, what resolves or changes, and how the product contributes. A process can reward through sustained sensory change; a demonstration can answer a worthwhile question; a story can reveal character through a specific choice. Category-level value can support an idea without an exclusive product capability. Conflict, reversals, voice-over, a CTA and a shock opening are not universal requirements.

## Let the communication difficulty shape the form

Identify what the audience needs to understand or feel and why the product is difficult to make interesting, credible or easy to discuss. Choose a demonstration, character, metaphor, sensory process or direct explanation that addresses that difficulty. A fictional device can make a subject approachable while the product explanation remains truthful. A memorable character or effect needs a meaningful product relationship, not a sales ending attached afterward.

Separate the proposition from its expression. Different locations, characters and jokes may express the same promise; compare different promises only when supported by the product and the brief. Do not polish dialogue as a substitute for choosing what the piece says. The opening creates an expectation the development and payoff must earn; audiences can infer meaning from choices and behavior without narration explaining every beat. Direct explanation is appropriate when it serves comprehension or purchase consideration.

Assess whether people would continue watching, associate the experience with the product and understand its value as separate questions. Entertainment, demonstration, brand cues and sound may do different work; no fixed reveal second, cue count, character requirement or call to action fits every brief.

## Compare directions when the brief is open

Explore a small number of materially different concepts rather than expanding the first one automatically. Differences should come from observation, point of view, viewing pleasure, the demonstration question or product relationship; new titles, professions or locations alone are not new concepts. If candidates still share the same formula, return to different available material or reframe the creative question before expanding them.

You may start from the strongest image, line or ending and work backwards to the expectation and development that make it land. Keep unexpected associations, humour and stylised expression when they serve the brief and retain a meaningful product relationship.

For each, describe the opening, key development and landing point in a few concrete sentences. Explain the creative difference; when recommending a direction, identify its specific strength and main creative trade-off. Use [concept diagnosis](references/diagnosis.md) for a vague payoff or product disconnect.

## Handoff and boundaries

Deliver the proposals and a brief recommendation. Pass the chosen direction, viewing motive, core event, payoff, product role and essential revelation order to the screenwriter. Carry user requirements and relevant product facts separately from adjustable creative choices. Continue to writing when the user requested a finished script; preserve explicit user selections.

Concept proposals remain conversation content unless the user requests a supported save destination. This Skill does not write screenplay records, shot records, model prompts or timelines, and grants no extra tool permissions. Actual dialogue, segment timing and the complete audiovisual script belong to the screenwriter; camera and staging belong to the shot director.
