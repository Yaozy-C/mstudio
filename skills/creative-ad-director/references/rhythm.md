# Action, cutting and sound rhythm

Use for shot timing, production handoff or feedback such as soft, dragging or flat. Choose the experience the film needs; do not turn every film into fast cutting. [Shot grammar](cinematography.md) owns camera and spatial relationships; [animation principles](animation-principles.md) owns poses, effort and inertia.

## Design the viewing process

Arrange contrasts in force, speed and pauses around events and recognition. Anticipation, acceleration, impact, hold and restart are options, not a required sequence or duration ratio. Within locked duration, redistribute time so essential information can be read.

In the existing shot plan, identify:

- In-shot rhythm: start, speed change, decisive contact/result, retained preparation and removable tail.
- Between-shot rhythm: action phase at entry/exit and how direction/attention carries. A cut need not wait for the previous subject to stop.
- Sound rhythm: accents on action, cuts or reveals; prelap, carry, reduction or silence; material-specific contact sound.

Flowing, energetic and beat-matched are not sufficient decisions. Long takes can vary internally; short equal shots can feel monotonous.

## Separate material from selection

Generate essential action phases and editing handles, not a complete dramatic arc in every clip. Generated length is not selected length. If action lacks acceleration, weight or response, repair design, reference or generation route; music and global speed changes do not repair it. If the action works but waits too long, trim entry/exit and repetition before considering local retiming. Check structure and readable contact after retiming. Slow motion requires viewing value, not the label cinematic.

## Sound in the rough cut

Use necessary temporary sound and music in the first authorized rough cut. Let music, action sound and ambience take turns leading. Loudness and density alone do not create impact. An in-shot action may land on a beat without a cut. Choose synchrony, anticipation, delay or cross-beat timing according to the scene; preserve essential contact/results.

## Verify and iterate

For uncertain pacing, compare two materially different rough cuts of the core section using existing footage and the same content/duration constraints. A clear small fix can be made directly. Comparison does not authorize new generation or concept changes.

Use available viewing modes to locate problems: silent viewing for waiting, floating, broken momentum and reading time; audio-only listening for dynamics, space and crowded accents; combined playback for material correspondence, contrast and value payoff. Each mode need not tell the entire story independently. Record actual timecodes, observations and changes. Missing contact calls for material repair, sync problems for alignment, redundant holds for trimming and overload for information restructuring. A comparison is an editorial judgment, not an audience test.

## Duration and speed feedback

Record whether time is a maximum, a user-locked total or an assistant estimate. Do not fill maxima or freeze estimates. Adding content should add progression, evidence or expectation, not repeated motion. If core content cannot fit a locked length, explain the concrete conflict.

Locate dragging before changing time: late start, slow action, repeated information, empty tail or insufficient recognition time require different fixes. Do not shrink every shot proportionally by default.

For an explicit whole-film multiplier r, apply it and set duration to original/r, synchronize audio and preserve pitch when required. Check subtitles and effects. An absolute speed differs from multiplying the current speed again. Do not turn a one-time multiplier into a permanent default; a simultaneous unchanged-total requirement needs conflict resolution.

Actual continuous playback of the new output establishes pacing. Stills can reveal wrong states or cuts, not natural playback speed. Without playback/audio access, retain unverified status.
