# Shot grammar: choose the shot from its purpose

Decide how viewers understand, anticipate and feel an event before choosing framing, staging, camera behaviour and cuts. Filling camera fields is not design.

## Purpose and viewpoint

Define each shot by an observable change: what viewers know on entry, what they discover, misread, anticipate or feel, and the visible event or spatial relationship that causes it. Purposeful echoes, pauses and repetition need no new selling point.

Decide whether viewers know before the subject, discover with it, or learn the cause after the result, and reveal targets, obstacles and outcomes accordingly. Do not expose a planned surprise by accident. Choose an observing, participating or restricted viewpoint for a reason; generation convenience never replaces a chosen viewing experience.

## Grammar within a shot

| Choice | Decide by |
|---|---|
| Shot size | Keep environment for geography and scale; show participants and contact for interaction; move in for decisive detail. A close-up loses context, so establish the relationship first. Do not open wide by reflex or alternate wide, medium and close mechanically. |
| Angle and viewpoint | Height and orientation serve the intended relationship, not a fixed emotional label. Check which surfaces are visible or occluded at the start, the key change and the end; one viewpoint cannot also see a surface available only from another side. |
| Staging | Give each beat one attention centre through silhouette, position, depth, contrast, motion and negative space. Secondary activity must not hide decisive contact. |
| Depth | Use depth for distance, approach, scale and trajectory. Foreground occlusion and shallow focus must not erase required evidence. State a compatible field of view and camera distance instead of conflicting lens jargon. |
| Static or moving camera | A hold emphasises change in the subject; movement follows action, reveals a relationship or changes participation. State the trigger, the relation to the subject and the destination. Not every shot moves. |
| On and off screen | Sound or a visible consequence can explain off-screen preparation. A process required as continuous proof stays visible; a cut cannot substitute. |

Distinguish camera translation, pan or tilt, optical zoom and post crop: translation changes perspective and foreground-to-background relationships, cropping does not. Rotating the product is not a camera orbit. For tracking, say whether the camera is fixed relative to, ahead of or behind the subject, and whether it keeps moving when the subject stops. A new product view needs structural evidence.

When an action depends on the product opening, closing, carrying or containing something, inspect its original structure first and anchor the path to verified edges, openings and attachments. If the action would need an unverified or contradictory mechanism, revise the staging and keep the purpose.

## Grammar between shots

- Geography: establish participants, targets and obstacles through a shared frame, landmarks, eyelines or continuous movement; give new orientation cues after a spatial change. A wide shot is optional.
- Axis and screen direction: establish the interaction or motion axis and keep meaningful left-right, eyeline and entrance-exit relationships. A deliberate axis crossing needs a readable crossing move, an on-axis shot, a re-establishment or an intended disorientation.
- Eyelines: a look and its target must agree with the intended spatial relationship; a reaction shot changes how the previous image is read. Under a no-face rule, use body orientation, a hand pause or a subjective view.
- Angle and size changes: small changes on the same subject create unintended jump cuts; roughly 30 degrees or a clear size change is the usual remedy, not a measured law. Keep deliberate jump cuts and graphic matches.
- Action matching: carry pose, direction, speed phase, held objects and contact across the cut; intentional time compression must remain understandable.
- Attention: know where attention leaves one shot and where the next receives it; a large shift needs reading time or guidance.
- Motivated cuts: cut when information, reaction, subject, space or rhythm has to change; hold when continuity proves a relationship or sustains immersion. Effects cannot hide missing causality.

Assess the sequence before polishing single shots. Repetition without progression, emphasis or an echo is merged or removed, not rescued by another angle.

## Motion and perceived speed

Specify subject motion and camera motion separately. For a speed-sensitive shot, decide what creates visible change: travel across the frame, approach and size change, passing foreground, or motion relative to another subject. A camera locked to the subject cancels its screen displacement; a fast world speed in the prompt does not make speed on screen. Choose framing, distance and environmental references for it; do not add shake or blur as a substitute for staging.

## Preserve the experience while repairing execution

Name the experience to keep: a restricted viewpoint, a darkness-to-light reveal, a scale reversal, an impact. The user's acceptance of that experience does not lock every measurement or operation you proposed. Surreal or miniature viewpoints are valid while perspective, occlusion and product structure stay consistent; accidental penetration or duplicated parts never are.

Locate the camera relative to stable parts and give its viewing direction. Check what is on screen, occluded or behind the camera as parts move. Give numbers only when they explain geometry or motion, with a reference and a unit; unsupported centimetres and exact timings create false precision.

Not every real-world operation must be fully visible: from inside an opening container, a change in the opening, the light and the sound can convey outside preparation. If a complete operation is essential evidence or explicitly requested, keep it and revise framing, staging or timing instead of cutting away from the chosen reveal.

Resolve alternatives into one compatible design in the shot text. Design review checks expression, compatible geometry and causal cuts; actual frames check execution; speed, performance pauses and synchronisation need motion and audio evidence. An unexpectedly good result may be adopted only when it keeps the core task and the user's constraints.
